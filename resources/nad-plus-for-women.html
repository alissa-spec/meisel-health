NAD+ for Women Over 40: What It Does and How It Works

Alissa Meisel

Board-Certified Health Coach · Published April 3, 2025

TL;DR

NAD+ is a coenzyme your body needs for cellular energy production, DNA repair, and cognitive function. Levels drop significantly with age, and that decline may contribute to the fatigue, brain fog, and slower recovery many women experience after 40. Supporting NAD+ levels through IV therapy, injections, or precursor supplementation is one of the foundational strategies in longevity medicine.

What NAD+ Actually Is

NAD+ stands for nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. It is a coenzyme, meaning it is a molecule that helps enzymes do their jobs. And those jobs are not optional. They are the fundamental operations that keep every cell in your body running.

NAD+ is involved in over 500 enzymatic reactions. It is essential for converting the food you eat into ATP, the energy currency of your cells. It activates sirtuins, a family of proteins involved in DNA repair, inflammation regulation, and cellular aging. It plays a direct role in circadian rhythm regulation, which is why NAD+ decline often shows up as disrupted sleep before anything else.

Here is the simplest way I can put it: if your cells are engines, NAD+ is the spark plug. Without enough of it, nothing runs efficiently. And after 40, most women do not have enough of it.

NAD+ and Cellular Energy

The fatigue that hits in your forties is not laziness. It is not "just stress." For many women, it is a mitochondrial problem, and NAD+ is at the center of it.

Your mitochondria, the powerhouses inside your cells, rely on NAD+ to convert nutrients into usable energy. When NAD+ levels are adequate, this process runs smoothly. When they are not, energy production slows down across every system in your body. Not just your muscles. Your brain, your immune system, your ability to recover from a workout, your capacity to think clearly at 3 PM instead of reaching for sugar.

This is not theoretical. Research has demonstrated that NAD+ is a rate-limiting factor in mitochondrial energy production. When you support NAD+ levels, you are supporting the most fundamental process your body uses to generate the energy it needs for everything else.

Cognitive Benefits and Brain Fog

If brain fog is the thing that scares you the most about aging, you are not alone. And NAD+ may be part of the conversation you need to have.

Your brain is the most metabolically active organ in your body. It uses roughly 20% of your total energy despite being only 2% of your body weight. When NAD+ levels decline, the brain feels it first. Slower processing, difficulty concentrating, the frustrating feeling of walking into a room and forgetting why you are there.

NAD+ supports cognitive function through multiple mechanisms. It fuels the mitochondria in your neurons. It activates sirtuins that are involved in neuronal health and neuroprotection. And it supports the repair of oxidative damage that accumulates in brain tissue over time.

Many women I work with report that improved mental clarity is one of the first things they notice when they begin supporting their NAD+ levels. Not overnight. But within weeks, the fog starts to lift. The word retrieval gets easier. The afternoons stop feeling like you are thinking through mud.

Why NAD+ Declines With Age

By the time you are in your forties, your NAD+ levels may be roughly 50% of what they were in your twenties. By sixty, the decline is even steeper. This is not something you did wrong. It is a fundamental feature of aging biology.

The decline happens for several reasons. First, the enzymes that produce NAD+ become less efficient with age. Second, an enzyme called CD38 becomes more active as you get older, and it consumes NAD+ at an accelerating rate. Third, chronic inflammation, which increases with age, further depletes NAD+ stores.

For women specifically, the hormonal shifts of perimenopause and menopause add another layer. Declining estrogen may affect mitochondrial function and increase oxidative stress, both of which increase the demand for NAD+ at exactly the time your body is producing less of it. This is why so many symptoms of menopause and symptoms of NAD+ decline overlap: fatigue, brain fog, disrupted sleep, slower recovery, changes in body composition.

Delivery Methods Compared

Not all NAD+ delivery methods are equal. Here is what you need to know about each one.

IV infusion delivers NAD+ directly into your bloodstream, bypassing the digestive system entirely. This is considered the most bioavailable method. Sessions typically take 2 to 4 hours and are administered in a clinical setting. Many women report feeling a noticeable difference in energy and clarity within 24 to 48 hours of their first infusion.

Subcutaneous injections offer a more convenient option that can often be self-administered at home after initial training. Bioavailability is lower than IV but significantly higher than oral supplementation. This is a practical middle ground for women who want consistent NAD+ support without the time commitment of regular IV sessions.

Oral precursors such as NMN (nicotinamide mononucleotide) and NR (nicotinamide riboside) are the most accessible option. They provide the raw materials your body needs to produce NAD+ on its own. Bioavailability is lower and the conversion process is not perfectly efficient, but for women who are not ready for injections or IV therapy, high-quality precursors may still provide meaningful support.

Nasal sprays are a newer delivery method that bypasses the digestive system through the nasal mucosa. Research is still emerging on the bioavailability of this route, but early results are promising for women who want a non-injection option with better absorption than oral supplements.

The right method depends on your goals, your budget, and what your provider recommends based on your specific situation. There is no single best answer. But there is a best answer for you, and that is worth a real conversation.
